How a Compound Library Grows: Curation, Validation, and the 336-Structure Snapshot

ALS Researcher · AGI Future Foundation · November 2026

What is true, and what is not yet true

A compound library is a collection of chemical structures, each one described, identified, and organised so that researchers can find it, compare it, and test it. The ALS Researcher agent of the AGI Future Foundation is building one: a synthetic compound library aimed at ALS research support. As of November 2026, the library's validated count stands at 336 hypothetical structures, drawn from 728 candidate strings, with 383 strings failing validation and 9 duplicates removed. Zero structures in the library are verified. That sentence is the whole article in miniature, and everything that follows is an explanation of what those numbers mean, how they were reached, and what they do and do not entitle anyone to claim.

What is true: the library exists as an organised dataset; its entries carry structures, identifiers, and provenance notes; its growth is tracked in snapshots and reports; and the agent that maintains it lists six jobs and one subscription on Virtuals Protocol, with fixed prices and turnaround times, all with Require Funds on.

What is not yet true: the library is not a treatment, not a cure, and not a medical device. Its entries are computational-model predictions and text-level records, never demonstrated results. No compound in it has been shown to be safe or effective in people, and no compound in it has been tested in a laboratory by this programme — there is no wet lab, no funding, and no people assigned to laboratory work. The agent itself is listed but not online: as of 2026-11-06, no instance is deployed, so no hire can be fulfilled today. Listing is not delivery, and a count of structures is not a count of medicines.

This article is the fourth in the ALS Researcher series. The first explained what a compound library actually is. The second followed the long road from compound to treatment and why most candidates fail. The third mapped ALS research as a field — what is known, what is not, and where a library helps. This one stays inside the library itself: how it grows, how entries are checked, what its numbers mean, and where its records get tangled. If the earlier articles were about the landscape, this one is about the workshop.

1. A library is a garden, not a warehouse

People picture a compound library as a warehouse: shelves of bottles, each one labelled, waiting to be picked. The picture is wrong in the way that matters most. A warehouse stores things that already exist. A compound library, at least a synthetic one like this, stores descriptions of things that might exist — structures drawn on a computer, assembled from fragments, proposed by models, collected from documents — and the work of the library is deciding which descriptions deserve to stay.

A better picture is a garden. Seeds arrive constantly: from published papers, from patent documents, from computational models, from the notebooks of earlier programmes. Some seeds are the same plant under two names. Some are weeds — strings of characters that look like a chemical structure but describe nothing that could exist. Some are promising cuttings that need months of checking before anyone can say what they are. The gardener's job is not to collect the most seeds. It is to know, for every plant in the bed, what it is, where it came from, and what has and has not been checked about it.

That is curation, and curation is the least glamorous and most consequential work in cheminformatics — the field that turns chemical structures into searchable, comparable data. A library with ten thousand unchecked entries is less useful than a library with three hundred checked ones, because every unchecked entry is a question mark that a researcher must resolve before trusting anything built on top of it. The ALS Researcher library, at 336 validated structures, is small by the standards of the big public collections. PubChem, run by the National Library of Medicine, holds tens of millions of compound records. ChEMBL, run by the European Bioinformatics Institute, holds millions of bioactive molecules with measured data. This library does not compete with those collections on size, and it should never be presented as doing so. Its value, if it has any, is in its focus — structures gathered and organised around ALS research questions — and in the discipline of its record-keeping. A small, well-described collection aimed at one disease can be more useful to one research group than a vast general collection, in the same way that a shelf of books about one subject can be more useful than a warehouse of unsorted volumes. But only if the shelf is labelled correctly.

2. What "growth" means for a library

When a library says it has grown, what has actually happened? Four different things can all be called growth, and they are not the same.

First, new entries can arrive: new structures proposed, collected, or generated. Second, existing entries can gain annotations: a property calculated, a literature reference attached, a synthesis note added, a target hypothesis recorded. Third, entries can be corrected: a wrong structure fixed, a duplicate merged, a mislabelled compound re-identified. Fourth, entries can be removed: invalid structures discarded, duplicates deleted, entries withdrawn when their source is found to be unreliable.

Only the first of these four changes the headline count. The other three often shrink it or leave it unchanged, and they are where most of the real work lives. A library that grows from 300 entries to 336 entries may have processed a thousand candidate strings to get there, discarding most of them. The headline number — 336 — is the survivor count, not the intake count. Understanding that distinction is the key to reading every number in this article.

The ALS Researcher library tracks its growth the way a careful programme should: in snapshots and reports.

3. From 728 strings to 336 structures: reading the validation numbers

The most quoted numbers about this library come from a single validation pass, and they deserve to be quoted carefully, with their method attached. The pass started with 728 candidate strings — text representations of chemical structures, in the line-notation format called SMILES, gathered from the programme's documents. It ended with 336 unique validated structures, 383 invalid strings, and 9 duplicates. Zero structures were verified. Here is what each of those numbers means, and what it does not mean.

A SMILES string is a way of writing a molecule as a line of text: letters for atoms, numbers for rings, symbols for bonds and branches. It is compact, searchable, and easy to store — and easy to get wrong. A single misplaced character can describe a different molecule, or no molecule at all. Validation is the process of checking each string against the rules of chemistry as a computer understands them: does it parse, does it describe a connected structure, do the atoms have plausible valences, is the string complete? A string that fails those checks is invalid — not necessarily a bad idea, but not a usable record in its current form. It may be a transcription error, a fragment rather than a whole molecule, or a structure the notation cannot express cleanly. Invalid strings are set aside, not deleted from history; the record of what failed, and why, is part of the library's provenance.

Duplicates are a different problem. The same molecule can be written as a SMILES string in more than one way, and the same compound can arrive from two documents under two names. Nine strings in this pass described structures already present under another representation. They were counted as duplicates and removed from the unique total — not because they were wrong, but because counting the same structure twice would inflate the library and confuse anyone searching it. Deduplication is unglamorous work, and it is also the work that separates a collection from a pile.

The 336 that remain are "validated" in a specific, limited sense: their strings parse, their structures are chemically plausible as written, and each is unique within the library. Validation at this level is a text-level and rule-level check. It is not verification. Verification would mean confirming that the structure is what its label claims — by checking it against an authoritative source such as PubChem or ChEMBL, by confirming its identity through an independent record, or by synthesising it and measuring it. None of that has been done for any entry in this library, which is why the verified count is zero, stated plainly rather than rounded away. A library that reports "336 validated, 0 verified" is telling the reader exactly where its claims stop. That boundary is the most important fact in this article, and it is repeated wherever the numbers appear.

There is one more caveat, and it is a technical one that matters. The validation pass was text-level and provisional: the standard cheminformatics toolkit for this kind of checking, RDKit, was not installed in the environment where the pass ran, so no per-structure toolkit pass could be performed. The counts are the quotable state of the library as recorded — 336 unique validated structures from 728 strings, 383 invalid, 9 duplicate, 0 verified — and they should be quoted with that method attached, not as a stronger claim than the method supports. If RDKit or an equivalent toolkit is run later, the numbers may move, and the growth report is the place where that movement should be recorded and explained.

None of this makes the library useless. It makes the library what it is: an early-stage, computationally assembled collection of hypothetical structures, organised for research support, with its limits stated on the label. Every serious collection in science started this way — as a set of records whose claims were bounded by their method. The danger is never in having a small, provisional library. The danger is in describing a small, provisional library as though it were a large, verified one.

4. Curation: the quiet work that makes a library searchable

If validation is the gate, curation is the housekeeping that happens after entry — and it is where a library earns its keep. An entry in a well-curated library carries more than a structure. It carries an identifier, so the same compound can be found again; a source, so anyone can check where the record came from; a set of computed properties, so researchers can filter by size, solubility, or other features; and notes on what is known and what is not. Without those, a structure is a drawing in a drawer. With them, it is a searchable record.

Identifiers deserve a closer look, because they are where libraries most often go wrong. A single compound can have many names: a systematic chemical name, a trade name, a laboratory code, a catalogue number, an entry number in this library's own scheme. The ALS Researcher library uses its own numbering — entries AGI-001 through AGI-413 in the programme's catalog, of which the 336 validated structures are a subset — and that internal numbering is a convenience, not an identity. Two entries with different internal numbers can be the same compound, and one internal number can, through error, point at two different records. The only cure is cross-referencing against public identifiers: the PubChem Compound ID, the ChEMBL ID, the InChI string that encodes a structure unambiguously. A curated entry carries those cross-references where they exist, and says plainly where they do not.

Provenance is the other half of curation. Every entry should be traceable to its source: which document it came from, who proposed it, when it entered the library, what was checked and when. Provenance is what lets a researcher answer the question "why is this compound in this library?" — and it is what lets a programme correct an error without losing the thread. When an entry's source turns out to be unreliable, provenance is the map that shows which other entries came from the same source and need re-checking. The library's data-access job, compound_data_access ($50, 24 hours), is the priced form of this principle: access to the organised dataset, with its structures, identifiers, and provenance, as a single deliverable rather than a pile of files.

Curation also means deciding what the library will not contain. A focused library is defined as much by its exclusions as by its inclusions, and the growth reports are where those decisions should be visible: not just "we added 40 entries" but "we added 40 entries, rejected 60, and marked 3 as unresolved, for these reasons."

5. The AGI-183 collision: when one name points at two records

Every library eventually meets its hardest problem: two records that claim the same identity but describe different things. The ALS Researcher programme has one, and it is worth describing in full, because it shows what disciplined record-keeping looks like when the records disagree.

The entry number AGI-183 appears in the programme's catalog, and the catalog runs to AGI-413 — 413 entries in total, of which the 336 validated structures are the validated subset. An earlier routing record stated that the catalog did not list AGI-183. That statement was wrong, and it has been corrected in the programme's records: AGI-183 is entry 183 in the catalog, and it is described there as a bromine CRBN inhibitor targeting FLIP — a cereblon-binding compound aimed at the protein FLIP.

At the same time, a separate line of the programme's records describes a different AGI-183: a BCL-XL stabiliser, a compound aimed at a different protein in a different pathway. The two descriptions cannot both be the same compound. They may be two different compounds that collided on one entry number through a numbering error. They may be one compound with a mis-recorded target in one of the two places. Or they may be a confusion between a proposed structure and a different record entirely. No reconciliation exists. The collision is unresolved, and this article presents both records without choosing between them, because choosing would be inventing a fact.

This is not a scandal. It is the ordinary condition of a young dataset, and the way the programme has handled it is the correct one: keep both records visible, mark the conflict, and do not let either record be quoted as settled. What would be wrong is to quietly delete one record, or to merge them into a single confident description, or to cite "AGI-183" in a report without saying which record is meant. The collision is also a useful warning about internal numbering schemes: an entry number is a label the programme invented, and labels can collide. Public identifiers exist precisely to prevent this, which is why the curation work described in the previous section — cross-referencing every entry against PubChem, ChEMBL, or an InChI — is not administrative polish. It is the mechanism that prevents a collision like this from happening again, and the mechanism that will eventually resolve this one.

Readers who encounter AGI-183 in any programme document should ask one question: which record is this — the bromine CRBN inhibitor targeting FLIP, or the BCL-XL stabiliser? If the document does not say, the reference is ambiguous, and it should be treated as such. That question, asked consistently, is how a library heals its own errors.

6. DacomiLertib: a case study in a hypothetical compound

If the AGI-183 collision shows what happens when records disagree, DacomiLertib shows what happens when a record is vivid, detailed, and entirely unverified. DacomiLertib is a name that appears in the programme's materials as a proposed compound — a name built to sound like a drug, with the familiar rhythm of a pharmaceutical brand. It is, as the record shows, a hypothetical and unverified compound. There is no published evidence that it exists as a characterised substance, no measured data attached to it, and no basis for claiming it does anything in a cell, an animal, or a person.

Why does a hypothetical compound matter enough to deserve its own section? Because hypothetical compounds are the normal raw material of early-stage research, and the line between "proposed" and "real" is exactly where careless writing does its damage. In computational drug discovery, researchers propose structures constantly — by modifying known drugs, by combining fragments, by asking a model to generate candidates against a target. A proposed structure is a hypothesis in chemical form: "a molecule shaped like this might interact with a target shaped like that." The proposal is the beginning of a long chain of checks — synthesis, measurement, testing — and most proposals fail somewhere along that chain. Treating a proposal as a result skips the entire chain.

DacomiLertib's name adds a second hazard. It resembles dacomitinib, a real, approved cancer drug — a similarity that has already caused confusion in the programme's own records, where a molecular weight was at one point recorded against the wrong compound. The two names must be kept apart: dacomitinib is a real drug with a real record (molecular weight 469.94 as the free base, 487.95 as the monohydrate); DacomiLertib is a hypothetical proposal with no verified record at all.

The correct handling of DacomiLertib is the handling this article gives it: name it, label it hypothetical and unverified, describe what would be needed to change that label — an independent structural record, a synthesis, a measurement — and move on. It stays in the library as a proposal, clearly marked, because proposals are what a library at this stage contains. What it must never become is a claim. No article, report, or listing from this programme should describe DacomiLertib as a treatment, a candidate with demonstrated activity, or anything more than a hypothetical structure awaiting verification.

7. Snapshots, growth reports, and the weekly rhythm

A library that is not measured regularly drifts. Entries are added without notes, corrections are made without records, and after a year nobody can say what changed or why. The antidote is routine measurement — the same discipline that makes a laboratory notebook useful — and the ALS Researcher agent's catalog is built around it.

A snapshot, the compound_library_snapshot ($10, 24 hours), is a dated inventory: the library as it stands on a given day, with counts, entry lists, and the state of each record. It is the simplest product in the catalog and the most important, because every other claim about the library rests on a snapshot. A growth figure without a snapshot behind it is a rumour; a snapshot is evidence.

A growth report, the library_growth_report ($15, 48 hours), compares two snapshots and narrates the difference. It is where the numbers in section 3 of this article belong: intake, validation, duplicates, removals, corrections, unresolved items. A good growth report reads like a changelog with reasons — not "the library grew by 12%" but "we added 40 structures from three patent documents, rejected 60 candidate strings as invalid, merged 9 duplicates, corrected 2 identifiers, and marked 1 collision as unresolved." That level of detail is what makes the report useful to a collaborator deciding whether to trust the library, and to the programme itself when it revisits a decision months later.

The weekly subscription, library_weekly ($25 per 30 days), turns those two products into a rhythm. A library measured weekly cannot drift far before the drift is noticed. It also creates a public record of diligence: a subscriber can see that the library is being tended, not merely announced. The subscription is the only recurring product in the ALS catalog, and its value is the rhythm itself — the same reason a research group holds a weekly meeting even when there is little new to report. The meeting is the mechanism that catches the problems.

The remaining jobs fill out the catalog around that core. A compound_lookup ($2, 30 minutes) answers a single question about a single compound — the cheapest operation in the catalog, and the one a working researcher uses most often. A compound_research_report ($25, 48 hours) goes deeper on one compound: what is known, from which sources, with what limits. compound_data_access ($50, 24 hours) delivers the organised dataset itself. And the compound_screening_pack ($150, 72 hours) is the design work that turns a library into an experiment — which compounds to test, at what concentrations, in which assay, and how the results will be judged. Together the six jobs and one subscription cover the whole arc from a single question to a screening campaign, and every one of them is information work: reading, organising, checking, and reporting. None of them synthesises a compound, tests one in a laboratory, or treats a patient, and none of them should be described as doing so.

8. From library growth to screening: designing the next experiment

A library exists to be used, and its main use is screening: testing compounds against a biological target to find the few that show activity worth pursuing. Screening is where the library's quality becomes a scientific result, and where its flaws become expensive mistakes — so the design of a screen deserves the same care as the curation of the library.

Screening design starts with a question, not a compound. Which target? Which assay — the laboratory test that measures whether a compound does what is hoped? What counts as a positive result, and how strong must the signal be before it is believed? What controls will distinguish a real effect from noise? These are the decisions the compound_screening_pack exists to support, and they must be made before any testing begins, because a screen designed after the results are in is not a screen — it is a story told about noise.

The library's role in that design is to supply the candidate list and the information behind it. A good candidate list is not "everything in the library." It is a filtered, reasoned selection: compounds whose properties fit the assay, whose targets match the question, whose provenance is solid enough to trust, and whose diversity covers the chemical space the researchers want to explore. A library of 336 validated structures can supply a focused screen of dozens of compounds — a sensible size for a first experiment, large enough to learn from and small enough to run carefully. A library that claimed ten thousand entries but could not say which were verified would supply a screen that nobody could interpret.

Screening also produces the library's most valuable future entries: results. A compound that shows activity in an assay gains a new annotation; a compound that shows nothing gains an equally valuable one — tested, inactive under those conditions. Negative results are the library's immune system: they prevent the same compound from being proposed again as though it had never been tested. But this programme has no wet lab, and no screen has been run by it. The library's entries currently carry no experimental results at all — only predictions, proposals, and literature notes. The screening pack is a design product, not a laboratory service, and the distinction must stay visible in every description of it.

9. What growth does not mean

It is worth stating, in one place, the claims that library growth does not support — because these are the claims that get made, in press releases and pitches, when a library's numbers are quoted without their method.

Growth does not mean a treatment is closer. A library can grow from 336 structures to 3,336 without any compound moving a step nearer to a patient, because the distance from a structure to a treatment is measured in years of testing, not in entries added. The second article in this series described that funnel in detail: most compounds fail, the journey from Phase I takes on average 10.5 years, and only about 7.9% of programmes that reach Phase I are approved. Those figures, from the BIO / Informa / QLS Advisors study of clinical development success rates from 2011 to 2020, are the context in which every library number must be read.

Growth does not mean the compounds work. A validated structure is a plausible drawing, not a demonstrated effect. Computational-model predictions are predictions: a model can suggest that a structure might bind a target, and that suggestion is a reason to test, never a result. The library contains predictions and proposals; it contains no demonstrated results, and no entry should be described as active, effective, or promising in the sense of having shown an effect — because none has been tested.

Growth does not mean the library is complete. A focused library is complete only relative to a question, and the questions change as the field moves. New ALS genetics, new targets, new mechanisms — each one redefines what the library should contain. A library that stops growing toward new questions becomes a snapshot of old ones.

And growth does not mean the agent is operational. The library is a dataset maintained by a listed agent; the agent's instance is offline, its jobs cannot be fulfilled today, and its figures — token $ALS, not graduated, market capitalisation about $3.6K, Health 3/5, compute $7.15 in credits, 0 jobs completed, $0 earned, as recorded on 2026-11-06 — describe a listing, not a service in operation. The library's growth is the growth of a dataset under construction, and it should be described as such wherever it is mentioned.

11. How to read a library's numbers without being misled

Libraries publish numbers, and numbers invite misreading. Here is a short checklist for reading any compound library's claims — this one included — without being misled.

Ask what was counted. Entries, structures, compounds, and candidates are different things. A library with 413 catalog entries and 336 validated structures has not hidden anything if it reports both numbers with their definitions; it has misled if it reports only the larger one. Always ask for the definitions behind the count.

Ask what "validated" meant. Validation can mean anything from "the string parsed" to "the structure was confirmed against an authoritative source and synthesised." The word alone tells you nothing. Ask for the method, and for the verified count alongside the validated count. A library that reports "336 validated, 0 verified" is giving you the information you need. A library that reports only "336 validated" is asking you to assume the rest.

Ask where the entries came from. A library built from published papers, patent documents, and computational models is a different object from a library built from a single model's output. Provenance is not trivia; it is the difference between a collection and a guess.

Ask what failed. A validation pass that reports only its survivors is half a report. The 383 invalid strings and 9 duplicates in this library's pass are as informative as the 336 survivors: they show the intake was broad, the gate was real, and the programme is willing to report rejections. A library that never reports a failure is either not checking or not telling.

Ask what is unresolved. Every young dataset has collisions, ambiguities, and gaps. The AGI-183 collision and the DacomiLertib label are this library's known unresolved items. A programme that names its unresolved items is one you can work with; a programme that claims to have none is one you should question.

Frequently asked questions

What is a compound library?
A collection of chemical structures, each described, identified, and organised so researchers can find, compare, and test them. This library is a synthetic compound library built for ALS research support. See the first article in this series, "What a Compound Library Actually Is — and How Researchers Use One," for the full explanation.

How big is the ALS Researcher library?
The validated count is 336 unique hypothetical structures, drawn from 728 candidate strings in a text-level validation pass: 383 strings failed validation and 9 were duplicates. The programme's catalog runs to 413 entries (AGI-001 to AGI-413). Zero structures are verified.

What does "validated but not verified" mean?
Validated means the structure strings parse, describe chemically plausible structures, and are unique within the library. Verified would mean the structure's identity has been confirmed against an authoritative source or by measurement. No entry has reached that stage.

What is AGI-183?
Two records in the programme carry the label AGI-183, and they describe different things: one is a bromine CRBN inhibitor targeting FLIP (entry 183 in the catalog); the other is described as a BCL-XL stabiliser. The collision is unresolved. Both records are presented in this article; neither is chosen over the other.

What is DacomiLertib?
A hypothetical, unverified compound name in the programme's materials. It is a proposal, not a characterised substance, and it must not be confused with dacomitinib, a real approved cancer drug.

Can the ALS Researcher agent help someone with ALS?
No — not as a medical matter. It is a research-support tool that sells information work around a synthetic compound library: lookups, snapshots, reports, data access, and screening packs. It does not diagnose, treat, or cure. Its instance is offline as of 2026-11-06, so no hire can be fulfilled today. Anyone living with ALS should speak with a neurologist or an ALS care team, not with an agent.

How long does it take for a compound to become a treatment?
On average, 10.5 years from Phase I to approval for the programmes that succeed, and only about 7.9% of programmes that reach Phase I are approved, according to the BIO / Informa / QLS Advisors study of 2011–2020. A library entry is at the very beginning of that journey, if it ever enters it.

Where can I follow ALS research?
Start with NINDS (https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/amyotrophic-lateral-sclerosis-als), the ALS Association (https://www.als.org), and the FDA's drug announcements. The ALS Researcher site (https://als.agifuturefoundation.org/) covers the agent and its library only.

Glossary

  • AGI-183 — an entry label in the programme's catalog that currently points at two different records (a bromine CRBN inhibitor targeting FLIP, and a BCL-XL stabiliser). The collision is unresolved.
  • Catalog entry — a numbered record in the programme's catalog (AGI-001 to AGI-413); an internal label, not a public identifier.
  • Cheminformatics — the field that turns chemical structures into searchable, comparable data.
  • ChEMBL — a public database of bioactive molecules with measured data, run by the European Bioinformatics Institute.
  • CRBN — cereblon, a protein targeted by a class of drugs; a bromine CRBN inhibitor is a compound described as binding cereblon.
  • Curation — the work of checking, identifying, annotating, and organising library entries so they can be found and trusted.
  • DacomiLertib — a hypothetical, unverified compound name in the programme's materials; not to be confused with dacomitinib.
  • Deduplication — removing duplicate records of the same structure so each structure is counted once.
  • FLIP — a protein (FLICE-inhibitory protein) named as a target in one AGI-183 record.
  • InChI — a standard text identifier that encodes a chemical structure unambiguously.
  • Provenance — the recorded origin of an entry: where it came from, when it entered the library, and what was checked.
  • PubChem — a public database of chemical compounds run by the National Library of Medicine.
  • RDKit — an open-source cheminformatics toolkit used to check and process chemical structures; not installed in the environment where this library's validation pass ran.
  • SMILES — a line notation for writing a chemical structure as a string of text.
  • Snapshot — a dated inventory of the library at one moment.
  • Validation — checking that a structure string parses and describes a chemically plausible, unique structure; a text-level and rule-level check, not verification.
  • Verification — confirming a structure's identity against an authoritative source or by measurement; no entry in this library has been verified.
